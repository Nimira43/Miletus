# 🏁 **Conclusion — Demo 1 vs Demo 2 (Pros & Cons)**

## ⭐ Demo 1: Median Filtering & Despeckling — *Global Behaviour*

### ✔ Pros

- **Fully automatic** — no threshold tuning required  
- **Robust to impulsive noise** — median filters naturally suppress spikes  
- **Simple to implement** — one sliding window, no spike detection logic  
- **Smooths noise everywhere** — good if the entire signal is noisy  
- **Preserves edges better than mean filters**

### ✖ Cons

- **Applies filtering to *every* sample**, even clean ones  
- Can **blur** the underlying signal if the window size is too large  
- Computationally heavier than targeted replacement  
- No distinction between “normal” and “spike” — everything gets processed  
- Not ideal when spikes are rare but the rest of the signal is clean

### 🧠 When to use Demo 1  

Use global median filtering when noise is **dense**, **frequent**, or **spread across the whole signal**.

---

## ⭐ Demo 2: Threshold‑Based Spike Detection & Local Median Replacement — *Targeted Behaviour*

### ✔ Pros

- **Only modifies corrupted samples** — clean data stays untouched  
- **Preserves the underlying signal extremely well**  
- **Very fast** — only a small number of indices are processed  
- **Highly effective for rare, high‑amplitude spikes**  
- Easy to tune visually via histogram inspection  
- No risk of global blurring

### ✖ Cons

- Requires **threshold selection**, which may be data‑dependent  
- Misses spikes if the threshold is too high  
- Can falsely classify normal peaks as spikes if threshold is too low  
- Assumes spikes are **much larger** than the real signal  
- Not suitable for continuous noise or low‑amplitude corruption

### 🧠 When to use Demo 2  

Use targeted median replacement when spikes are **rare**, **isolated**, and **much larger** than the underlying signal.

---

## 🥇 **Which is better?**

It depends entirely on the noise characteristics:

### ✔ If spikes are **rare and huge** → **Demo 2 wins**  

It preserves the signal perfectly and only fixes the corrupted points.

### ✔ If noise is **continuous or frequent** → **Demo 1 wins**  

It smooths the entire signal and handles widespread corruption.

### ✔ If you want **maximum signal preservation** → **Demo 2**  

Only spike points are touched.

### ✔ If you want **fully automatic behaviour** → **Demo 1**  

No threshold tuning required.

---

## 🔧 Final Recommendation

For the current synthetic dataset — where spikes are:

- random  
- sparse  
- extremely large  

**Demo 2 is the superior despeckling method.**

But both demos are essential because they teach:

- global vs targeted filtering  
- linear vs non‑linear behaviour  
- thresholding vs neighbourhood statistics  
- how different filters respond to impulsive noise
